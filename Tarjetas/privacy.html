Privacy Policy for Tarjetas

Last updated 23 September 2026

The short version

Tarjetas works on your device. There is no account to open, no password and no email address. It sends nothing anywhere until you use the half of it that needs somebody else — the classroom or the friends page — and what it sends then is only what has to reach them: the name you type, the emoji you pick, the code people find you by, and the things you deliberately hand over — a class you publish, a friend request you send, a dictionary you give a friend.

The app arrives already pointing at a server, so that those two pages work without anybody having to be told an address. Being pointed at one is not being on it: opening the app writes you nowhere, and drilling your own words never does.

Three things can leave your phone without either of those. If you say an answer, or words for your dictionary, aloud instead of typing them, your phone's own speech recognition may use the network to hear them — see Saying an answer aloud. On Android, reading a word list off a photo or a screenshot uses Google's ML Kit, a library inside the app, which tells Google how its reading went — never the picture, and never the words on it. And if you turn Recognition to "In the cloud" in the settings, a photograph is sent to OCR.space to be read there instead of on the phone. That switch is off unless you turn it on, and it is the only thing in the app that sends a picture anywhere — see Reading a photo or a screenshot.

There is no advertising and no tracking of any kind, and the app keeps no analytics of its own. Nothing about you is sold or profiled, and nothing is shared beyond what this page describes.

What stays on your device

  • Your words, their translations, and how you have grouped them
  • Your drill progress — what you have learned, what is due, and when
  • Your settings: display name, avatar emoji and colour, whether you are a student or a teacher, the server address if you have entered one, and the code you are found by. If you have used more than one server, the code and the proof that it is yours are kept for each, so that pointing the app back at an earlier one finds the account you already had there
  • Classes you have made or joined, with their lessons
  • Friends, requests you have sent or received, and dictionaries friends have sent you

None of this is sent to the developer or to anybody else. Uninstalling the app removes all of it from the device — see Backup below for the one copy that is not on the device and is not the developer's either.

What is sent to a server, and when

Only when you use the half of the app that needs somebody else. Tarjetas arrives already pointing at a server — the one described under Where the data is kept — so that the classroom and the friends page work without anybody having to be told an address.

Being pointed at it is not being on it. A device that has never claimed a code is not allowed to claim one on a sync nobody asked for, so opening the app writes you nowhere. Your code is claimed the first time you open the friends or classroom page, add somebody, or publish or join a class — the half of the app that needs other people, and the only half that needs a code at all. Somebody who installs Tarjetas, drills their own words for a year and never opens those two pages has no record on the server at all.

The Server field on the Settings page holds that address and it is yours to change. A teacher running their own server types its address over the top, and this policy stops describing where the data goes — see A server somebody else runs. Emptying the field stops all contact with any server: every screen still works, and nothing about you is sent to one.

When any of it is sent, this is sent — and nothing else:

Your profile. The code you are found by, the name you have typed, your avatar emoji and its colour, and the date you last opened the app. The date is kept for one purpose: so a teacher can see which of their students has used the app lately. It is a date, not a time.

Friends. When you ask somebody or accept a request: the two codes, which of you asked, whether it was accepted, and the date. Somebody you have only asked does not see your name or your emoji until they say yes.

Classes you publish, as a teacher. The class name and code, your name and emoji as its teacher, and each lesson with its words, translations, notes and homework. The group emoji you file your own words under is deliberately removed first — how you organise your own dictionary is not part of the lesson.

Classes you join, as a student. Your code, the class code and the date, so the teacher can see who is in the room — and so can the other students in it, who see your name and emoji. The date you last opened the app is shown to the teacher alone. Somebody you have blocked, or who has blocked you, is not shown to you there, and you are not shown to them. Once the teacher takes you out of a class, only the teacher still sees you listed in it.

Students you take out of a class, as a teacher. Their code, the class code and the date, so the class stops reaching them. Their own phone is told, so they see that their teacher removed them rather than wondering why nothing new arrives. The lessons they already have stay on their phone.

A key on a class, as a teacher. If you put a key on a class, the server keeps it beside the class, so that knowing the code is no longer enough to join. It is never handed out with the class: your students do not receive it, and the server gives it to nobody. Making a new key replaces it, and taking the key off deletes it. A student who types the key keeps it on their own phone, so that the phone can get back into the class by itself.

Homework you hand in, as a student. Your answer as you wrote it, your code, the class code, the lesson it answers and the time you handed it in. Only the teacher of that class can read it; it is not shown to the other students. Handing in again replaces the answer. It is deleted when you delete your account, when the teacher deletes the lesson or the class, or when the teacher deletes their account. While you are out of the class — you left, or the teacher took you out — the teacher does not see it. Your answers are also kept on your own phone, so you can read and correct them without a connection. An answer you have written but not yet handed in stays on your phone and is sent nowhere.

Answers you check, as a teacher. The mistakes you mark in an answer — which words, and what you wrote about each — and the time you marked it checked, kept with the answer. Only the student who wrote it receives them, and their phone keeps a copy so they can read them without a connection. Checking again replaces them. They are deleted when the student hands in a different answer, and whenever the answer itself is deleted. What you write about a mistake goes through the same filter as your class notes.

Quizzes you write, as a teacher. Each question, its options or accepted answers with the right ones marked, and the time each question is open, kept on the server beside the class but apart from it. Your students' phones are told only how many questions a quiz has and how long each is open until you run it; while it runs, each question is shown without its answer until its time is up; and a student is sent the whole quiz only once they have taken it, so they can take it again on their own phone. Questions and answers go through the same filter as your class notes. Once a quiz has been run, the server keeps its questions as they were, because everybody's results are held against them. It is deleted when you take the quiz off, or delete the class or your account.

Quizzes you take, as a student. Your answer to each question, when you gave it, what it scored, and whether you took the quiz with the class or later on your own. When the quiz is over, everybody in the class sees the results — each student's name, emoji and score — and your teacher sees whether you took it late. While a quiz is waiting to start, the students who have joined see each other's names. A student who is no longer in the class is shown to the teacher alone. It is deleted when you delete your account, or when the teacher takes the quiz off or deletes the class or their account. Taking a quiz again afterwards happens on your phone, and nothing about it is sent.

Dictionaries you share. When you send your dictionary to a friend: the words and translations in it, both codes, and the date. The group emoji you file your words under is removed first, exactly as it is from a lesson — how you arrange your own dictionary is yours and stays on your device. It waits on the server until your friend takes it, or until either of you removes it.

People you block. When you block somebody: the two codes and the date. Nobody is told they have been blocked, and the list is readable by you alone — it is on your Settings page, which is also where you undo it.

Reports you make. When you report a person, a class or a dictionary somebody has sent you: your code, theirs, which of those three it was, the reason you picked, and a copy of the text you were looking at — a display name, a lesson note, or the words in the dictionary — up to two thousand characters of it.

That copy is the part worth being plain about, because it is the one thing in this app that sends somebody else's writing somewhere on your say-so. It is sent because a report without it cannot be acted on: by the time anybody reads it the note may have been edited and the dictionary taken back, and what was on your screen is the only copy left. It goes nowhere but the server, it is read by the person who runs it and nobody else, and the person you reported is not told and is not shown what you wrote. Reporting needs a server, so on a device with the Server field emptied the app says so rather than offering the form. Blocking never does.

Saying an answer aloud

When typing is switched on in the drill, the microphone button beside the answer field lets you say the answer instead. The app asks for the microphone the first time you press that button and not before, and the microphone is on only while the button is lit. A right answer moves you on to the next card and the microphone stays on for it; it goes off at the first answer that is not right, after a few seconds with nothing said, when you press the button again, or when you move the card on yourself.

The dictionary has a microphone too, on its page for adding many words at once. There you say a word in Spanish and then its translation, in the language picked beside the button, and each pair is written onto the page as a line of text for you to check. The microphone is on only while that button is lit; it goes off after a few seconds with nothing said, when you press the button again, or when you leave the page or the Dictionary tab. Nothing goes into your dictionary until you add the lines yourself.

What you say is turned into text by the speech recognition your phone already has — on Android usually Google's, on iOS Apple's — and the text is put in the answer field, or on the page, exactly as if you had typed it. It is checked there like anything typed, and it is not sent anywhere.

Where your phone has speech recognition installed for the language being heard, it does this on the phone itself. Where it has not, the phone's speech service does it over the network, under Google's or Apple's policy. Tarjetas does not record, keep or send the sound, and none of it reaches the developer or the Tarjetas server.

Reading a photo or a screenshot

The dictionary's page for adding many words has a camera button too. It opens your phone's own camera app, and the photo you take is read on the phone: the lines of text found on it are written onto the page for you to check, exactly as if you had typed them. Nothing goes into your dictionary until you add the lines yourself. The photo is deleted as soon as it has been read, and Tarjetas sends neither the photo nor the text read from it anywhere. A few camera apps also keep a copy of every photo they take in your gallery; that copy is the camera app's, and it can be deleted there.

Beside it, a picture button opens your phone's own picture picker, for a screenshot or a photo you took earlier. Tarjetas is given only the picture you pick, and only to read it: it asks for no access to your photos, sees none of the others, and neither copies nor keeps the one you picked. It is read exactly as a photo is.

On iOS the reading is done by Apple's Vision, which is part of iOS, and nothing about it leaves the phone.

On Android it is done by Google's ML Kit, a library built into the app, which reads on the phone and needs no network. ML Kit does report to Google on its own work: the phone's make, model and system version, the app's name and version, the reader's version, the picture's format and resolution, how much was read and how long it took, error codes, and an identifier for this installation of the app that is not meant to identify you or your phone. It does not send the picture or the words read from it. Google says it sends this over an encrypted connection, uses it for diagnostics and usage statistics, and passes it to nobody else — see Google's description of it and Google's privacy policy. None of it reaches the developer or the Tarjetas server.

Reading in the cloud, which is off unless you turn it on. The settings page has a Recognition switch with two ends: this phone, which is how the app arrives and everything above describes, and the cloud. Turn it to the cloud and a photograph is no longer read on the phone: it is sent, as a JPEG of up to a megabyte, to OCR.space, which reads it and sends back the text. Nothing else goes with it — no name, no code, no account, nothing that says which phone or which person it came from, because the app has none of that to send. The service answers with text and the app puts it on the page for you to check, exactly as a photograph read on the phone is. OCR.space says it does not store the documents it is sent; their terms are at ocr.space/privacypolicy.

Turning the switch back to this phone stops it, and nothing sent before that can be taken back — a picture once sent has been sent. If you would rather no photograph ever left the phone, leave the switch where it arrives.

The key it is read under. OCR.space wants a key with each picture. The app carries one, on a free plan the developer registered and pays nothing for, shared by every copy of the app; it is good for a fixed number of pictures a month across everybody, and when that is used up the service says so and the reading fails until the month turns. You can put a key of your own in the settings instead — OCR.space gives them away at ocr.space/ocrapi — and then your pictures are read under your key and not the shared one. The key is kept on your phone and sent to OCR.space and to nobody else. The developer cannot see which pictures were read under either key, only how many the shared one has left.

What is never collected

No email address, password, phone number, real name, date of birth, location, contacts, photos, calendar, payment details, recordings of your voice, device identifier or advertising ID.

No crash reporting, no advertising, no social network integration, and no analytics of the app's own. Two third parties can receive anything at all, both described under Reading a photo or a screenshot: Google's ML Kit in the Android app, which reports on its own reading and never the picture; and OCR.space, which receives a photograph itself, but only where you have turned the Recognition switch to the cloud, and never anything saying whose it is. Apart from those, the app talks to one server, which is the one whose address is in its settings, and to nothing else. Saying an answer or a word aloud uses your phone's own speech recognition, as described above, not a service of the app's.

Permissions

Android — Internet. Used only to reach the server address in the settings, and only for what is described above.

Android — text-to-speech visibility. A declaration that lets the app see the speech engine already installed on your phone. Speaking happens on the device; nothing is sent anywhere to do it.

Android — microphone. Asked for the first time you press a microphone button, in the drill or on the dictionary's page for adding many words, and used only while that button is lit, to hear an answer or a word said aloud. Refusing it changes nothing else in the app.

Android — speech recognition visibility. A declaration, like the text-to-speech one, that lets the app see the speech recognition already installed on your phone.

Android — camera app visibility. A declaration, like the two above, that lets the app see the camera app already on your phone, which is what takes the photo.

Android — document picker visibility. Another such declaration, for phones older than Android's photo picker: it lets the app see the system's document picker, through which a picture is picked there.

Android — network state. Added by Google's ML Kit, which looks at whether the phone is online before sending its report. It is granted without asking, and it shows nothing of what you do online.

iOS — local network. Asked for only when the server address is on the same network as your phone, which is what happens when a teacher runs one on a laptop in the classroom.

iOS — microphone and speech recognition. Both asked for the first time you press a microphone button, in the drill or on the dictionary's page for adding many words, and used only while that button is lit, to hear an answer or a word said aloud. Refusing either takes the button away and changes nothing else in the app.

iOS — camera. Asked for the first time you press the camera button on the dictionary's page for adding many words, and used only while the camera is open. Refusing it takes the button away and changes nothing else in the app.

The app requests no location, contacts or storage permission, no access to your photos on either platform — a picture is picked through the phone's own picker, which hands over only that one — and on Android no camera permission either: the photo is taken by your phone's own camera app.

Backup, and your account on a new phone

Tarjetas allows your phone's own backup, which is on by default on both platforms: Android's auto-backup to your Google account, and iCloud to your Apple account. That takes the app's data with it — your words, your progress and your settings.

This is deliberate, and it is what carries an account to a new phone. Your settings are where the code people find you by is kept, so restoring a backup brings back the same code, the same friends and the same classes. There is no password here and no email address, so without the backup there would be nothing else that could hand any of that back.

The backup is made by your phone into your own account, under Google's or Apple's policy and your own backup settings — not by this app, and not by its developer, who has no access to it and no way to ask for it.

You can turn it off in your device's settings. If you do, a phone that is wiped or replaced comes back as a new user with a new code, and nobody can restore the old one — not the developer, and not the server. The same is true if you move between an Android phone and an iPhone, since neither backup can be read by the other.

Where the data is kept

The server this app is shipped pointing at is a virtual machine in the European Union, rented from netcup GmbH. Its database is a single file on that machine, copied continuously to Cloudflare R2 object storage, also held within the European Union. No one else is given access to either.

Deleting it

From inside the app: Settings → Account → the bin. It asks first, and shows you what will go. Confirming removes, from the server: your code and profile, every friendship and every unanswered request in either direction, the classes you published together with their student lists and the students taken out of them, every class you were taken out of, every shared dictionary to or from you, every block you have made and every block made against you, and every report you have made and every report made about you. It cannot be undone.

The reports and blocks go in both directions on purpose, and it is the one part of this that is not obvious. A code nobody holds is handed out again to the next device that asks for one, so a block or a report left behind naming your old code would attach itself to whoever next has it — somebody who has done nothing.

Your words and your drill progress are not touched by this — they were never on the server. Neither are the classes on your own device: a class you made stays where you wrote it, with its lessons, the same way your dictionary does. What deletion takes from it is everybody else's way in. It is off the server, its code is registered to nobody, no one can join it and its student list is gone. Deleting an account is meant to stop you being findable, not to take a term of lesson planning with it. Uninstalling the app removes those.

What deletion cannot reach is a copy somebody else already has. A friend who has taken the dictionary you sent them is holding those words on their own device; a student who has merged your lesson into their own list is holding that. A class you published stays on the phones of the students who joined it, with the name and emoji you had when you published — it simply stops receiving anything new. Deleting your account removes what the server held, and stops any of it being handed out again — it does not travel to the phones where it has already landed. Worth knowing before you rely on it, because "delete my account" reads to most people as "and everything that came from me", and for what has already been given away it is not.

Nothing is kept after deletion, and there is no archive to ask about. If you would rather ask a person to delete it for you, write to the address at the foot of this page.

Your rights

You can see everything held about you from inside the app — the friends list, your classes and your profile are the whole of it. You can correct your name and emoji at any time on the Settings page, and you can delete everything as described above. If you would rather ask a person to do any of this, write to the address below.

A server somebody else runs

Tarjetas lets a teacher enter the address of their own server, which is what the Server field is for. If you do that, this policy does not describe where your data goes: it goes to whoever runs that machine, under whatever terms they offer. The app itself behaves identically either way — what changes is who is holding it.

Children

Tarjetas is not directed to children under 13 and is not listed for them. An account is not knowingly opened for anybody under that age, and where we learn one has been, it is removed.

Where a teacher uses the app with a class that includes younger pupils, they do so as the adult running the room. The same removal applies either way: a teacher or a parent who wants a child's data removed can do it from inside the app with the bin described above, or write to the address below and it will be done.

It is built to need as little about a person as possible: no email address, no password, and no real name — the name is whatever you type into the field, and a first name or a nickname is enough. There is no advertising, no tracking and no profiling of anybody, of any age.

Changes to this policy

If what the app collects ever changes, this page changes with it, and the date at the top changes too. The app is not updated to collect something new without this page saying so first.
